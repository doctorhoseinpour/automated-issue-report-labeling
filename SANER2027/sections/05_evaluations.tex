\subsection{RQ1: Classification with Retrieved Examples}
\label{sec:rq1}

We compare \rag\ with two reference points. Zero-shot prompting ($k{=}0$) is the LLM without examples and reaches 61.3--68.8\% macro $F_1$. The \knn\ baseline is retrieval without the LLM. We evaluate it at $k\in\{1,\ldots,20,25,30\}$ to find how many neighbors still add label information (\Cref{fig:kcurves}a). Its macro $F_1$ rises slowly with $k$ and peaks at 59.5\% with the target project's issues ($k{=}15$) and at 60.4\% with all projects' issues ($k{=}16$); no larger $k$ scores higher. These peaks set the top of \rag's $k$ grid.

The pooled index helps the vote only slightly. PA scores higher than PS at every $k$, but by little, because even the pooled index returns mostly neighbors from the query's own project; PS and PA share 84--90\% of their top-$k$ neighbors. The two settings therefore give the vote, and later the LLM, nearly the same neighbors. \knn\ also over-predicts bug and is weakest on questions, labeling 32\% of them as bugs.

Without examples, the LLMs over-predict bug. Zero-shot prompting assigns the bug label to about half of all issues, although a third are bugs, and labels 46--59\% of questions as bugs but far fewer as features. These questions make up 76--84\% of its false bug predictions, so low question recall and low bug precision are two sides of the same error.

All 48 configurations with $k{\ge}1$ (four sizes, six values of $k$, two settings) outperform both reference points in macro $F_1$, macro precision, and macro recall (\Cref{fig:kcurves}b). Even one example raises macro $F_1$ at every size. The best $k$ grows with model size, from 3 at Qwen-3B and 6 at Qwen-7B to 12 at Qwen-14B and Qwen-32B, giving 69.7--76.7\% macro $F_1$. With examples, macro $F_1$ grows with every step in model size, whereas zero-shot prompting does not (Qwen-14B scores below Qwen-7B). Beyond their best $k$, the two smaller models lose macro $F_1$ as examples are added, while the two larger ones stay nearly flat; no model improves from $k{=}12$ to $k{=}15$. We hypothesize that larger models make better use of long contexts with many examples. Retrieving from the target project scores slightly higher than retrieving from all projects at most sizes and values of $k$, as the neighbor overlap leads us to expect, so we use PS for \rag\ and \frag\ in the rest of the paper.

Most of \rag's gain comes from questions. At the best $k$, the examples add 5.5--8.7 points to zero-shot prompting, and the rise in question $F_1$ accounts for 56--70\% of this gain. The examples lower the share of questions labeled as bugs to 26--36\%, and with it the share of issues labeled as bugs, which raises bug precision along with question recall. Question nevertheless keeps the lowest per-class $F_1$ at every $k$, and questions labeled as bugs remain the largest source of false bug predictions (74--79\%). RQ2 targets this error.

\begin{myboxi}
\rag\ outperforms both zero-shot prompting and \knn\ at every number of examples, reaching 69.7--76.7\% macro $F_1$ at its best $k$ (3 to 12). The gain comes mostly from questions, but 26--36\% of questions are still labeled as bugs.
\end{myboxi}

\subsection{RQ2: Filtering the Retrieved Examples}
\label{sec:rq2}

\Cref{fig:kcurves}c compares \frag\ with \rag\ at the same $k$. \Frag\ scores higher at every $k{\ge}6$ for all four sizes and lower at $k{\le}3$. The margin explains the small values of $k$. With $m{=}3$ and at most three neighbors, $N_\text{bug}-N_\text{question}\le 3$ holds for every query, so the filter removes the bug-labeled examples unconditionally. It leaves 40\% of the prompts at $k{=}1$ and 18\% at $k{=}3$ without any example, which turns these queries into zero-shot prompts.

At each method's best $k$, \frag\ is 1.5--2.4 points higher than \rag, and the CIs of all four differences exclude zero. Its best $k$ is larger at the three smaller sizes (6, 12, and 15 instead of 3, 6, and 12) and unchanged at Qwen-32B, and like \rag, it gains little or nothing beyond $k{=}12$. When the filter fires, it removes every bug-labeled example from the top $k$, so a larger initial $k$ is needed to leave enough examples in the prompt.

The gain is a targeted reduction of question-to-bug errors. The share of questions labeled as bugs falls from 26--36\% to 19--27\% at the best $k$, and it also falls when averaged over all values of $k$, so the effect does not hinge on the choice of $k$. Question recall rises by 6.5--15.3 points, while question precision falls by 1.2--5.0 points. The precision loss comes mainly from bugs. The filter also fires for many true bugs (\Cref{sec:approach-bragtag}), and the share of bugs labeled as questions rises at every size.

On the bug label, the filter trades recall for precision. Bug precision rises and bug recall falls at every size. The loss is largest at Qwen-3B, where bug recall drops from 80.3\% to 66.5\% and bug $F_1$ drops by 1.8 points, the only size at which the filter over-corrects; at the three larger sizes, bug $F_1$ rises. At Qwen-3B, the share of bugs labeled as questions nearly doubles, far more than at the larger sizes, which suggests that the smallest model follows the remaining examples most closely. Feature $F_1$ changes by at most 1.1 points, so the filter leaves the feature label almost untouched (\Cref{tab:results} gives all per-class values).

\begin{myboxi}
\Frag\ outperforms \rag\ at every $k{\ge}6$, by 1.5--2.4 points at each method's best $k$. It lowers the share of questions labeled as bugs to 19--27\%, at the cost of bug recall.
\end{myboxi}

\subsection{RQ3: RAG versus LoRA Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:rq3}

\myparagraph{Same labeled issues}
We fine-tune the four models in both settings (\Cref{tab:results}). Given the same labeled issues of the target project, both RAG variants outperform fine-tuning at every size, \rag\ by 2.1--4.0 points and \frag\ by 3.8--6.0 points. Fine-tuned on these issues, the models still improve on zero-shot prompting at every size, but less than with retrieved examples. We hypothesize that 300 issues are too few for fine-tuning to learn the task well, whereas \rag\ needs only the few that are most similar to each query. The large gain that fine-tuning draws from more training issues supports this.

Pooling the labeled issues of all eleven projects raises fine-tuning's macro $F_1$ by 3.1--8.4 points, consistent with Heo and Lee~\cite{heo2025study}. Pooling changes both the number of training issues and their project mix, and its effect differs by label; at Qwen-32B, for example, it raises bug recall but lowers question recall. Pooling does not help \rag, because the pooled index returns mostly the same neighbors (\Cref{sec:rq1}). We therefore compare each method in its better setting: the RAG variants with the target project's issues (PS) and fine-tuning with the pooled issues (PA). The eleven PS indexes together hold the same 3{,}300 issues as the PA training set, so the settings differ in the labeled data available per target project, not in the total labeling effort.

\myparagraph{Pooled fine-tuning}
Averaged over the four sizes, \frag\ trails fine-tuning by 1.0 point, and the CI includes zero; \rag\ trails by 2.8 points, and the CI excludes zero (\Cref{tab:method-comparison-ci}). The filter thus turns a significant deficit into a difference that is not significant. Per size, \rag\ trails fine-tuning everywhere, significantly at Qwen-7B and Qwen-14B. Fine-tuning is significantly ahead of \frag\ at Qwen-7B and Qwen-14B, while \frag\ is ahead at Qwen-3B and Qwen-32B, but not significantly. Fine-tuning's significant leads fall at the two sizes where pooling helps it most, by more than 8 points, against about 3 points at Qwen-3B and Qwen-32B.

Each method's best $k$ is chosen on the test set, which favors the RAG variants, since fine-tuning has no $k$ to select. To check how much this matters, we fix $k{=}12$ for every size. \Frag\ then trails fine-tuning by 1.2 points on average instead of 1.0, and it remains ahead at Qwen-32B.

Results also vary by project (\Cref{fig:per-project-diff}). \Frag\ matches or exceeds fine-tuning in 16 of the 44 (project, model) pairs, including all four sizes on \texttt{opencv} and three on \texttt{bitcoin}, while fine-tuning leads at all four sizes on \texttt{TypeScript}. Differences on single projects reach about 10 points in either direction, so the parity in aggregate does not hold for every project.

\myparagraph{Labeled data, memory, and time}
\Frag\ uses $11\times$ less labeled data per target project than pooled fine-tuning and has no training phase. For each target project, fine-tuning learns from 3{,}300 labeled issues, whereas the RAG variants retrieve from that project's 300. Both RAG variants need 33\% less peak GPU memory on average (\Cref{tab:method-cost}), comparing their inference with fine-tuning's training and inference of the same model. The saving ranges from a quarter at Qwen-14B to almost half at Qwen-3B, although the RAG prompts may be four times as long as fine-tuning's (\Cref{sec:finetune}).

The longer prompts make inference slower. Fine-tuned models label the 3{,}300 test issues in 0.10--0.44~h, and \frag\ in 0.22--4.15~h. Including training, \frag\ takes less total time than fine-tuning at Qwen-3B and Qwen-14B, about the same at Qwen-7B, and more at Qwen-32B. These totals include a single training run. Fine-tuning must be repeated whenever new labeled issues are to be learned or the model is replaced, whereas retrieval only indexes the new issues and uses any LLM as is. Conversely, fine-tuning's faster inference pays off more, the more issues are labeled between two training runs. Without an LLM, \knn\ labels all test issues in under 4~s with 0.2~GB of GPU memory.

\myparagraph{Error profiles}
Fine-tuning over-predicts bug, as zero-shot prompting does (\Cref{sec:rq1}), although its training data are balanced across labels. It labels 39--46\% of the test issues as bugs, although 33\% are bugs. It therefore finds more of the actual bugs than \frag\ at every size, with bug recall of 86.5--92.9\% against 66.5--80.4\%, but it also makes more false bug predictions at three of the four sizes. The exception is Qwen-14B, where fine-tuning's over-prediction is weakest. Per label, fine-tuning has the higher bug $F_1$ at every size, whereas \frag\ has the higher question $F_1$ at Qwen-3B and Qwen-32B, the two sizes at which it leads in macro $F_1$.

The question-to-bug confusion persists under both approaches, with 23--36\% of questions labeled as bugs after fine-tuning and 19--27\% after \frag. Our failure analysis (\Cref{sec:disc-failures}) shows that most of the sampled Qwen-32B errors of the three LLM approaches are issues filed under a bug-report template. Which error profile fits a project better depends on whether a missed bug or a false bug label costs it more.

\myparagraph{Invalid outputs}
Invalid outputs make up 1.8--4.7\% of \frag's outputs and at most 0.8\% of fine-tuning's. In a sample, most of them continue the issue body or the model's reasoning instead of giving a label (\Cref{sec:disc-failures}). An invalid output counts as a missed issue of its true label but adds no prediction to any label's precision, so it lowers macro recall but not macro precision. \Frag's macro precision is within 1.4 points of fine-tuning's at every size, while its macro recall is lower at Qwen-7B and Qwen-14B, where 6--8\% of true bugs receive invalid outputs. We hypothesize that the long prompts, filled with issue text, lead the model to continue that text rather than answer.

To compare complete pipelines that give every issue a valid label, we let \knn\ label the invalid outputs of both methods. The RAG variants use PS voting at $k{=}15$ over their unfiltered neighbors, and fine-tuning uses PA voting at $k{=}16$. The fallback needs no extra LLM call; the RAG variants reuse their neighbors, and fine-tuning gains a retrieval step. With the fallback, \frag\ is 0.1 point ahead of fine-tuning on average, and the CI includes zero. Its deficits at Qwen-7B and Qwen-14B shrink to within 1.4 points and are no longer significant, and it becomes significantly ahead at Qwen-32B. Fine-tuning's scores change by at most 0.3 points, since it has few invalid outputs, and \rag\ still trails it significantly on average.

\begin{myboxi}
With only the target project's labeled issues, no training, and 33\% less peak GPU memory on average, \frag\ comes within 1.0 point of the macro $F_1$ of fine-tuning on the labeled issues of all eleven projects, a difference that is not statistically significant; \rag\ trails by 2.8 points. Given the same labeled issues, both RAG variants outperform fine-tuning. Their inference is slower, and fine-tuning finds more bugs but over-predicts the bug label.
\end{myboxi}
