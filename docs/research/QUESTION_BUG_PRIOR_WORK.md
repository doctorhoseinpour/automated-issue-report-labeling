```latex
% Prior-work context for the question-to-bug confusion (ESEM reviewer C).
% Evidence, quotes and page numbers: docs/research/QUESTION_BUG_PRIOR_WORK.md
\myparagraph{\fixed{Question-to-Bug Misclassification.}} \fixed{In \irc\ studies that include both bug and question labels, question is often the hardest class to identify, and misclassified questions are most often labeled as bugs. For example, almost all methods in the NLBSE'24 tool competition struggled with question~\cite{kallis2024nlbse}, and on the same dataset, the two best-performing zero-shot LLMs labeled 46--54\% of questions as bugs~\cite{colavito2025benchmarking}. Fine-tuned decoder LLMs also score lowest on question in aggregate~\cite{aracena2025applying, heo2025study}. Our results show the same pattern. Every method we evaluate misclassifies questions as bugs, with \votag\ predicting bug for 32\% of questions, zero-shot prompting for 46--59\%, and PA fine-tuning for 23--36\% (\Cref{sec:rq1,sec:ragtag,sec:method-comparison}). As a remedy, Aracena et al.~\cite{aracena2025applying} suggested retrieval augmentation, particularly for ambiguous labels such as question. Our RAG-based methods show that it helps but does not completely eliminate the problem. Without any training, retrieved examples lower the share of questions predicted as bugs from 46--59\% to 26--36\%, and \bragtag\ lowers it further to 19--27\%.}


\fixed{Prior work offers several explanations for why questions are misclassified as bugs. A common reason is label imbalance, since question is often the smallest class in the training data~\cite{izadi2022catiss, bharadwaj2022github}. Another explanation is that some questions read like bug reports because they describe unexpected behavior, contain error messages~\cite{colavito2024impact}, or lack explicit interrogative markers~\cite{aracena2025applying}. Our failure analysis (\Cref{sec:disc-failures}) shows the same effect for questions filed under a bug-report template, which led the model to predict bug regardless of their content. Finally, a label can depend on information outside the report, such as a maintainer's later diagnosis~\cite{colavito2024impact}. Likewise, the label-noise cases in our failure analysis have labels that do not match their content and cannot be inferred from the report text alone.}
```

```latex
\myparagraph{Retrieval-Augmented Few-shot Prompting.} Studies show that few-shot prompting can improve LLM performance in specialized tasks due to in-context learning~\cite{assi2026llm, le2023log, ma2023fairnessguidedfewshotpromptinglarge, logan2021cuttingpromptsparameterssimple, brown2020language}. Few-shot prompting has also been applied in IRC. Colavito et al.~\cite{colavito2025benchmarking, colavito2024leveraging} randomly selected one or two labeled examples \fixed{per class from their training data} and presented them in a few-shot prompt. \fixed{In their benchmark, these examples did not improve over zero-shot prompting for most models~\cite{colavito2025benchmarking}.} Few-shot performance is sensitive to the quality of in-context examples. Research shows that using retrieval techniques to select the most semantically relevant in-context examples can outperform random selection~\cite{liu2022makes, yu2023retrieval}. Since retrieval-augmented few-shot prompting can enhance performance without the overhead of training, it can be a promising alternative to the LoRA fine-tuning IRC baseline. \fixed{Recent IRC studies also point in this direction in their future work. Heo and Lee~\cite{heo2025study} suggest few-shot prompting to improve accuracy across diverse projects, and Aracena et al.~\cite{aracena2025applying} propose retrieval augmentation, in which relevant past issues are retrieved and added to the LLM's prompt, to reduce the need for extensive fine-tuning.} To the best of our knowledge, however, this method has not been systematically and empirically evaluated for IRC.
```

## 7. Step 3 reasoning (brief)

**Placement.** The text started as a Discussion subsection. Because the reviewer's objection is about how the confusion is *introduced*, cited clauses were added where the paper first mentions it (§I and §II-D). The rest went through two layouts before the current one:
- A split moved the prior-work findings into one Related Work sentence. That sentence had no lead-in, because Related Work never discussed per-class results, so it was removed and the Discussion subsection was restored.
- At the user's request, the text was then restructured and moved to the end of Related Work, which now follows the Discussion. Related Work can therefore refer back to our results (§IV) and the failure analysis (§V-A), and each proposed cause is followed directly by our observation.

**What is established.** Question is the weakest class, and question→bug is its dominant error. This has been documented for several model families:
- fine-tuned encoders: RoBERTa, with 27% of questions labeled bug (JSS Table 8); question is also the weakest class for every readable NLBSE'22 entry;
- every approach in the NLBSE'24 competition, encoders and generative models alike (NLBSE24 Table 1, §2.4);
- zero-shot decoder LLMs, with 46–54% on NLBSE'24 and 23–28% on the manually verified NLBSE'23 set (IST Tables 8–9);
- fine-tuned decoder LLMs, whose aggregate question F1 is the lowest (SCP; ICPC).

It is not universal:
- Ticket Tagger (fastText, balanced 2018 data) does not have question as its weakest class.
- On manually verified four-class data, documentation is the weakest class for most models (§5).
- Per project, question is not always last.

Hence the wording "known weakness" rather than "universal".

**Where our results agree.**
- Our zero-shot rates are 46–59%; IST's are 46–54% on five of our projects.
- Our fine-tuned models still send 23–36% of questions to bug on balanced data.
- Retrieval reduces the rate but does not remove it.

**Where comparisons break down.** The text never compares magnitudes across these differences:
- JSS: imbalanced (question 9%), "enhancement" instead of "feature", input truncated to 128 tokens.
- IST NLBSE'23: four classes and verified labels.
- All studies: different models and prompts.
- Test sets overlap only in part: 5 of 11 projects, and about half of NLBSE'24's test issues (755 of 1,500) are in our test split.

**What prior work offers as explanations, and what our results say about each.**
- **Imbalance.** JSS undersampling lowers the rate, so imbalance matters on imbalanced data. Our balanced benchmark and NLBSE'24 still show the confusion, but the models may have learned from pretraining that bug is the more frequent label, so this does not rule imbalance out. The text now states imbalance without an observation of ours; the zero-shot sentence (R3) was cut.
- **Bug-shaped questions with error messages** (JSS). Supported by our template category.
- **Missing interrogative markers** (SCP). Not tested: our failure analysis did not code interrogatives.
- **Labels settled later by maintainers** (JSS). Consistent with our label-noise and hybrid-intent cases, but not tested, since we did not read maintainer comments.
- **Inconsistent labeling rationales across projects** (IST). Not in the text: nothing in our failure analysis speaks to it, and our design cannot isolate it: PS and PA retrieve largely the same neighbors ([05:34](../../SANER2027/sections/05_evaluations.tex#L34)), and PA fine-tuning beats PS fine-tuning.

**What works in our favor.**
- Three points are in the text: the cross-method view including voting without an LLM (F1, F8); a proposed remedy that we test (F4); and the match with a documented cause (F10). F2 (imbalance is not sufficient) was removed.
- The strongest-sounding claim, that RAG methods beat prior approaches on question, is refuted by Heo & Lee's GPT-4o fine-tune on this very benchmark (F7).
